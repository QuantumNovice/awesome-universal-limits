# How hot and how cold can things be?

![Temperature vs size](../../figures/temperature.png)

**Hard bounds.** At the Planck temperature (1.4×10³² K), the energy of a typical thermal photon would make it collapse into a black hole. Known physics stops there (red).

Absolute zero is the other hard bound. On a log axis it lies infinitely far down, so it can't be drawn. The third law of thermodynamics says it can be approached but never reached. The coldest atom cloud ever made, at 38 picokelvin, marks how close we have come.

**Reference lines.**
- *Black-body collapse (dashed grey).* Fill a sphere of diameter *d* with black-body radiation and it forms a black hole once T > (3c⁴/(2πGa d²))^¼. This applies only to radiation in equilibrium. Dilute hot gas, like the plasma in galaxy clusters, can be hotter than this line without collapsing, so the line is not shaded.
- *Hawking temperature (dash-dot).* The temperature of a black hole whose diameter is *d*. A 10 M☉ black hole glows at 6 nK, colder than any laboratory.
- *Highest melting point (dotted amber).* No known solid survives above about 4,200 K (hafnium carbide).

**Objects.** The chart runs from the cosmic microwave background and quark-gluon plasma (trillions of kelvin, in a space the size of a nucleus) to the Sun (photosphere to core), newborn and old neutron stars, Earth's surface extremes, lightning and the human body. The cap above the human marks lethal hyperthermia.
