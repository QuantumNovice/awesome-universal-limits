# How dense can things be?

![Density vs size](../../figures/density.png)

**Hard bound.** A black hole of diameter *d* has mean density 3c²/(2πGd²) inside its horizon. Nothing of that size can be denser (red). Because the bound falls as 1/d², big black holes are not dense at all: M87* averages about a third of the density of air. The bound crosses the mean density of the universe near the Hubble scale. That coincidence is a consequence of the Friedmann equation.

**Practical limits.** Nuclear density (2.7×10¹⁷ kg/m³, dashed grey) is the densest matter that exists outside a black hole. Atomic nuclei, protons and neutron stars cluster around it. The dash-dot line is the universe's mean (critical) density, for scale.

**Objects.** Intergalactic gas, the Milky Way's average, interstellar gas (bar: from hot ionised gas to dense molecular cores), air, Saturn (less dense than water), Earth, osmium, the Sun (bar: from photosphere to core), the white dwarf Sirius B, and the neutron star PSR J0740+6620.
