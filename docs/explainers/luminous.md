# How bright can a light be?

![Luminous intensity](../../figures/luminous.png)

Luminous intensity, in candela, measures how bright a source looks to the human eye in a given direction. The SI defines it so that 1 watt of 555 nm green light (where the eye is most sensitive) gives 683 lumens. No light source can do better per watt.

**Lines.**
- **The Sun's power, all at 555 nm (dotted amber).** The Sun's actual luminous intensity (2.8×10²⁷ cd) is about seven times below this, because much of its light is infrared or ultraviolet.
- **The Planck power at 683 lm/W (red).** Nothing can be brighter than about 2×10⁵⁴ cd.

**Sources.**
- A candle is about 1 cd; the unit grew out of standard candles. A household LED bulb shines at roughly 60–130 cd averaged over all directions.
- UN Regulation 48 caps a car's main beams at 430,000 cd in total.
- The full Moon, seen from Earth, shines at about 10¹⁶ cd.
- Stars and explosions are converted from their absolute visual magnitude M_V, scaled from the Sun (M_V = 4.81, 2.8×10²⁷ cd). By that measure Sirius A gives 6×10²⁸ cd, a Type Ia supernova at peak 10³⁷ cd and the quasar 3C 273 10⁴⁰ cd.
- The optical flash of GRB 080319B, the brightest light ever recorded, reached about 6×10⁴⁴ cd. It was visible to the naked eye from 7.5 billion light-years away, and it is still ten billion times below the Planck ceiling.
